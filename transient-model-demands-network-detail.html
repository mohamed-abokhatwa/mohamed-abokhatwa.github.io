Demands, Branches and Dead Ends in a Transient Model: What Skeletonisation Must Keep

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 13 min read

A transient model is a simplification of the network, and the simplifications are not neutral. On the 12 km reference main with its vessel, an offtake of 0.15 m³/s at 6 km changes the answer depending only on how it is drawn. As a fixed demand the line falls to +2.11 m and fails the +3.0 m criterion; as a pressure-dependent demand it holds +3.82 m; as a branch pipe to a tank, +5.57 m; left out altogether, +4.33 m. Add a closed 1.5 km branch to the main and the line minimum drops to +1.26 m while the dead end goes to vapour. This article shows which network details a surge model must keep, how to model demands, and what can safely be removed.

1 · Why network detail matters in a transient

A steady-state model only needs the network to carry the right flows at the right heads, so it tolerates heavy simplification. A transient model also needs the network to reflect, absorb and store pressure waves correctly [1][2]:

  • Every junction reflects part of a wave, and every branch sends part of it away and back later.
  • A dead end doubles the wave that reaches it and returns it to the main, delayed by twice its travel time.
  • Demands respond to pressure. A real offtake, tap or leak draws less when the pressure falls, which damps a downsurge; a fixed-flow demand keeps drawing, which deepens it.
  • Tanks and reservoirs on branches feed water back during a downsurge and absorb it during an upsurge.

The examples use the series' reference main: 12 km of DN800 ductile iron at a wave speed of 1,050 m/s, 0.70 m³/s entering at the pump, a delivery reservoir at 44.8 m, and the series vessel (3.5 m³ of gas, DN400 differential connection) protecting an instant pump stop [3]. The network solver is the same method-of-characteristics code with a vapour cavity model, verified against the series solver to 0.01 m.

2 · Fixed or pressure-dependent demand

The offtake at 6 km takes 0.15 m³/s, leaving 0.55 m³/s for the second half of the main. Three ways to model it are common [2][4]:

\[ \text{fixed: } q = q_0, \qquad \text{orifice: } q = q_0\sqrt{\frac{p}{p_0}}, \qquad \text{branch: a pipe to a tank, with its own wave speed and friction} \]

A fixed demand is the default in many models, because steady-state demands are usually entered that way. During a downsurge it keeps drawing 0.15 m³/s however low the pressure falls, so the main loses water it would in reality keep. The orifice form, sized to pass \(q_0\) at the steady pressure \(p_0\), draws less as the pressure falls and more when it rises. A real offtake to a tank is a pipe, and the tank supplies water back into the main when the main falls below it.

The three ways of modelling the same offtake give +2.11, +3.82 and +5.57 m: a fail, a narrow pass and a comfortable pass. The fixed demand is the most pessimistic, the branch to a tank the most favourable, because the tank feeds water back into the main at 6 km. The orifice demand also moves the lowest point: from 4.7 km to the pump. Leaving the offtake out (+4.33 m) is not conservative here: it overstates the margin of the fixed-demand case by 2.2 m.

The pressure-dependent demand falls from 0.15 to 0.069 m³/s as the downsurge arrives and rises to 0.18 on the return: it behaves as a small relief at the junction. The closed branch fills and empties with every reflection, and its far end goes to vapour 18 s after the trip, while the main loses 3 m of minimum.

3 · Dead ends and closed valves

A closed branch is the clearest example of a detail that skeletonisation must keep. A wave entering it reflects from the closed end with doubled amplitude and comes back to the main after \(2L_b/a\): 2.9 s for the 1.5 km branch here [1][5]. The returning wave adds to the main's own transient. In our case the closed branch, carrying no flow at all, takes the main from +4.33 to +1.26 m and its own far end to vapour. Fire mains, washout and future-connection stubs, and valves normally closed between zones are all closed branches during a transient.

4 · What can be removed, and what cannot

Skeletonising a network for a transient model: our rules
DetailKeep or removeWhy
Large offtakes and branches to tanksKeep as pipesThey reflect, feed back and change the result (section 2)
Closed branches, dead ends, normally closed valvesKeepThey double and return the wave (section 3)
Small distributed demandsLump into pressure-dependent demands at nearby nodesFixed demands overstate the downsurge
Short pipes and fittings in seriesMerge, keeping travel timeThey force a small time step (see time step)
Loops in a distribution networkKeep the main loopsA loop splits and recombines waves; a tree model can miss or exaggerate peaks
Devices, high points and changes of sectionAlways keepThey are where the answer is read

The test of any simplification is to run the governing scenario on the detailed and the skeletonised model and compare the envelopes at the critical points [2][6]. A skeleton that changes the minimum by more than the margin describes a different system.

5 · Setting it up in Bentley HAMMER

Field names differ slightly between versions [7].

  1. Start from the steady-state model and decide, branch by branch, what the transient model must keep, using the table in section 4.
  2. Use Skelebrator for series pipes and small branches, but lock the elements that must stay: offtakes to tanks, closed branches, devices, high points.
  3. Choose the demand representation for the transient: check how your version treats junction demands during a transient run, and use a pressure-dependent form (an emitter or orifice) for offtakes that really respond to pressure. Model large offtakes as pipes to their tanks.
  4. Keep normally closed valves as closed valves, so the branches behind them are dead ends in the transient.
  5. Run the governing scenario on both models, the detailed and the skeleton, and compare the envelopes at the critical points before using the skeleton for the protection design.
  6. Bracket the demand model: run fixed and pressure-dependent demands and report both if the verdict depends on it.

6 · Checklist

  • Every large offtake modelled as a pipe to its tank, or as a pressure-dependent demand.
  • Dead ends and closed valves kept, with their branches.
  • Small demands lumped into pressure-dependent demands, not fixed flows.
  • Skeleton checked against the detailed model on the governing scenario.
  • Demand model bracketed where the verdict depends on it.
  • Loops kept in distribution networks.
