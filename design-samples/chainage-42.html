CH 1+400Just past the station

A pump trip is not a slow event.

When the power goes, the pumps stop in about a second. The water does not. Forty-two kilometres of column keeps moving away from the station, and the pressure behind it collapses at Δh = a·v/g — here, a wave speed of 1,000 m/s against 1.06 m/s of velocity, so 108 metres of head, gone, travelling out along the line at the speed of sound in water.

It reaches the far end in 42 seconds and comes back. Everything that matters happens inside the 84-second round trip, which is why the steady-state calculation that sized the pump has almost nothing to say about what the pipe has to survive.

CH 28+000Summit · +198.0

Everything fails at the top of the hill.

At the summit the pipe rises to within 10 metres of the hydraulic grade. In steady running that is fine — one bar, a thin but perfectly legal margin. It is also the only place on forty-two kilometres where the line has nothing left to give.

Trip the pumps unprotected and the grade here drops 36 m, which puts the water 26 m below the crown of the pipe. That is not low pressure. That is a vapour cavity forming, growing while the column runs on, and then closing again when the column comes back — and the rejoining columns hit each other far harder than the original wave ever did.

The instinct is to stop the pumps more slowly. Work it out and the instinct dies: you would need 303 seconds of deceleration against a critical time of 84. No flywheel does that. The summit has to be protected by putting the water back — a vessel at the station, or air valves that let the cavity fill and then have to get the air out again without slamming.
