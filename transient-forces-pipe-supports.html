Transient Forces on Pipe Supports and Bends: From HAMMER Heads to Loads the Structure Can Take

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 14 min read

A surge program reports heads and flows; the pipe fails at its supports. When a pressure wave runs along a straight leg between two bends, the pressure at one end differs from the pressure at the other, and the difference acts on the pipe as an unbalanced axial force. On the 12 km DN800 reference main, a line valve closed instantly at the far end loads a 50 m above-ground leg with 733 kN for 48 ms. Close the valve in 5 s and the same leg sees 81 kN. With the series vessel protecting a pump trip, the leg next to the pump sees 17 kN. A support designed for the static thrust at a bend, or only for friction along the pipe, can take none of the first. This article shows how to turn the transient results into forces, how a support's natural frequency changes what it feels, and what to hand to the structural engineer.

1 · Two different forces

Pipe supports see two kinds of hydraulic load [1][2]:

  • Bend thrust. At every change of direction, pressure and momentum give a resultant that a thrust block, an anchor or a restrained joint must hold. It exists in steady flow and grows with the surge pressure: \(F_b = 2A\left(p + \rho v^2\right)\sin(\theta/2)\).
  • Unbalanced force on a leg. In steady flow the thrusts at the two ends of a straight leg between bends cancel along its axis, apart from friction. While a wave front is inside the leg they do not: one end is at the new pressure and the other at the old. The net axial force is \(F_L = \rho g A\,\big(H_1(t) - H_2(t)\big)\), and it lasts as long as the front takes to cross the leg, \(L/a\) [1][3].

The second is easy to miss, because a surge model draws envelopes along the line rather than differences between two points at the same instant, yet it is the load that shakes above-ground pipework off its supports, opens restrained joints and fails pipe racks in pump stations.

2 · Bend thrust at the surge pressure

For the reference main (DN800, \(A\) = 0.503 m²) a 90° bend carries 591 kN at the steady 85 m, 829 kN at the 119 m peak of the protected trip, and 1,322 kN at the unprotected 190 m. The momentum term is small at these velocities (about 1.4 kN). Thrust blocks and anchors are designed on the maximum pressure of the envelope at that bend, which is why the envelope must be read at every bend and not only at the pump.

3 · Unbalanced force on a straight leg

An instant closure sends the full Joukowsky front (149 m) into the leg: 733 kN whatever the leg length, lasting \(L/a\), from 48 ms on a 50 m leg to 0.19 s on a 200 m leg. A 5 s closure spreads the front over 5 km of pipe, so the head difference across a leg grows with its length: 81, 153 and 273 kN for 50, 100 and 200 m. The unprotected pump trip peaks at 521 kN when cavities near the pump collapse 47 s after the trip, a load no envelope shows. The vessel keeps the leg below 30 kN.

4 · What the support feels: dynamic load factor

A support with a natural period \(T\) does not feel a short pulse as a static load. For a rectangular pulse of duration \(t_d\) applied suddenly, the peak response of a single-degree-of-freedom system, as a multiple of the static response to the same force, is [5][6]

\[ DLF \;=\; 2\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi t_d}{T}\right) \quad \text{for } \frac{t_d}{T} \le \tfrac12, \qquad DLF = 2 \quad \text{otherwise} \]

and for a load that rises over a time \(t_r\) and then stays,

\[ DLF \;=\; 1 + \frac{\left|\sin(\pi t_r/T)\right|}{\pi t_r/T} \]

A suddenly applied load that lasts longer than half the support's period doubles its effect. A pulse much shorter than the period is felt only partly. A load that builds slowly compared with the period is felt as static.

A stiff 10 Hz support (T = 0.1 s) under the instant-closure pulse on a 50 m leg (48 ms) has td/T = 0.48 and a DLF of 2.0: the support must be designed for about 1,460 kN. A flexible 2 Hz support sees td/T = 0.1 and a DLF of 0.6, but it moves a long way doing so. For the 5 s closure most of the head rise comes in the last moments of the stroke, so the leg still sees a pulse (0.29 s on a 50 m leg), longer than a stiff support's period: the DLF stays at 2. The slower closure helps by making the force smaller, not the factor.

5 · What to hand to the structural engineer

  • Bend thrusts at the transient maximum pressure of each bend, with the steady value for comparison.
  • Leg force histories \(F_L(t)\) for every straight leg of above-ground or plant-room pipework next to a fast event (pump, check valve, control valve, line valve), for the governing scenarios.
  • Peak force, pulse duration and rise time for each, so a DLF can be applied, or the history itself for a time-history analysis.
  • The direction of each force along the leg, and whether it reverses (it usually does, as the wave reflects).
  • The event rate: a load that happens on every pump stop is a fatigue load for the supports, not only a strength case.

The cheapest fix is usually upstream: slow the closure (see control valve closure time), protect the trip (see choosing surge protection), or avoid long straight legs between bends next to fast valves. Burying or restraining the pipe moves the load into the soil and the joints, which must then be checked for it. The static design of above-ground pipe is covered in above-ground pipeline design.

6 · Getting the forces out of Bentley HAMMER

HAMMER reports heads, pressures and flows at nodes and along pipes; the force on a leg is a short calculation on two of them [7].

  1. Put nodes at every bend of the above-ground or plant-room pipework, so each straight leg is one pipe between two nodes, and give the short pipes their real lengths (see time step and adjustment: short pipes need care).
  2. Report every time step at those nodes, so the pulse is not thinned out.
  3. Export the head histories of the two end nodes of each leg, and form \(F_L(t) = \rho g A(H_1 - H_2)\) in a spreadsheet; add the bend thrusts from the maximum pressure at each bend.
  4. Check the pulse is resolved: the leg must be at least one reach long, and a halved time step must not change the peak.
  5. Hand over the peaks, durations, directions and histories with the scenario names.

7 · Checklist

  • Bend thrusts at the transient maximum, at every bend.
  • Unbalanced forces on straight legs next to every fast event, as histories.
  • Pulse durations and rise times given, with a DLF or a time-history analysis.
  • Collapse events included where the column separates: their forces are not in any envelope.
  • Supports, anchors and restrained joints checked for the peak, the reversal and the number of events.
  • Upstream fixes considered first: closure times, protection, layout.
