Booster Stations in Series: When One Trip Becomes a Surge at Both Stations

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 14 min read

On a long carrier with an in-line booster, a trip at one station is a surge event at both. We modelled station 1 pumping 8 km into the suction of an in-line booster that lifts the flow another 10 km to a reservoir. If the booster trips alone, station 1 keeps pushing and the suction main rises to 76 m. If station 1 trips and the booster runs on, the booster empties its own suction main and the pressure there reaches vapour in 9 s. If both trip, both mains separate. A break tank at the booster decouples the two mains, and then each main needs only its own protection: 4.2 m³ of gas at station 1 and 2.8 m³ at the booster keep both lines at or above +3 m in every trip. This article works through the scenarios, the interlocks and the choice between in-line boosting and a break tank.

1 · The system

The carrier is DN800 ductile iron with the friction gradient of the series' reference main, 0.70 m³/s and a wave speed of 1,050 m/s [1]:

  • Station 1 lifts from a 5 m suction to 60.0 m: 471 kW at 1,480 rpm, inertia 26.1 kg·m² from the correlation (see pump data).
  • Main A, 8 km, delivers to the booster at 33.2 m.
  • The booster adds 45.3 m: 388 kW, inertia 19.9 kg·m², with a check valve and a bypass check valve that lets flow pass the stopped pumps when the suction pressure exceeds the discharge pressure.
  • Main B, 10 km, delivers to a reservoir at 45.0 m.

The model is a method-of-characteristics network solver with a vapour cavity model, verified against the series solver to 0.01 m; the pumps run down on their inertia with the constant-efficiency torque law [2][3]. The profile is flat, so heads are pressure heads.

2 · Three trips, two mains

An in-line booster joins the two mains hydraulically: the flow through main A is the flow through the booster, and so through main B. Any change at either station reaches both mains [4][5].

  • Booster trips, station 1 runs on. The flow through the booster drops, main A decelerates against a station still pumping, and the suction pressure rises until the bypass opens. Main B sees an ordinary downsurge from a stopped pump.
  • Station 1 trips, booster runs on. Main A decelerates, but the booster keeps drawing from it. Its suction pressure falls until the water column separates at the booster and the pump runs into vapour.
  • Power failure at both. Both mains decelerate at once, and the booster's own rundown pulls its suction down further while main A is already falling.

In-line, the booster trip lifts the suction main to 75.7 m while main B dips to 30.1 m; the station 1 trip takes the booster suction to vapour after 9.0 s; the power failure separates both mains. With a break tank, each main sees only its own station: a station 1 trip affects main A alone (−9.3 m, close to vapour), a booster trip affects main B alone (+11.9 m). With a vessel at each station the worst minimum on either main is +3.2 m, and the vessel's return swing lifts main B to 129.8 m, inside the 136 m limit of this series.

3 · The low-suction interlock

Every in-line booster needs a low-suction trip, to stop it running into vapour when the supply fails. A common instinct is to delay that trip so that a brief dip does not stop the booster. The model shows what the delay does after a station 1 trip [5][6].

Whatever the delay, the booster suction separates: run on, the booster drains its suction in 9 s; trip it at once and its own rundown, combined with main A's downsurge, separates the suction after about 18 s. Main B reaches vapour in every case in which the booster stops. The interlock protects the pump from running dry; it does not protect either main. That needs protection on the mains, which is the subject of the next section.

4 · Decouple, then protect each main

Protecting an in-line pair is hard because every device interacts with both stations: a vessel at station 1 cannot stop the booster drawing its suction down, and a suction-side vessel at the booster changes the discharge-side response. Our sizing runs with three vessels on the in-line arrangement (station 1, booster suction, booster discharge) did not find a combination that passed all three trips. A break tank (or a reservoir) at the booster removes the coupling: main A discharges into the tank, and the booster lifts from it [4][7]. Only the decoupled system with a vessel at each station passes all three trips at +3.0 m. Lower the criterion to −3 m and nothing else changes: every other arrangement still reaches vapour in at least one trip.

The break tank brings its own costs: a structure, its overflow and level control, a second set of pumps drawing from atmospheric pressure, and the loss of the residual head at the booster (33.2 m here), which the booster must then supply. Where the head is too valuable to break, the in-line arrangement must be protected as one system, with suction and discharge devices sized together and checked against all three trips and the interlocks, usually with more conservative devices and faster interlocks.

5 · Points we check on every series system

  • All three trips: each station alone and both together. A power failure at one site is a common event on long carriers fed from different substations.
  • The booster suction as its own critical point, with its minimum against vapour and the pump's NPSH.
  • The bypass round an in-line booster, and its check valve, modelled; without it a stopped booster dead-ends main A.
  • Interlocks (low suction, high discharge, station-to-station signals) modelled with their real delays, and their purpose stated: protecting the pump, not the main.
  • Restarts: the order of restarting the stations, so that the booster never starts on an empty suction main.

6 · Setting it up in Bentley HAMMER

Field names differ slightly between versions [8].

  1. Model both stations and both mains in one model, with the booster's pumps, check valves and bypass explicit, and its suction node at the real elevation.
  2. Give each pump its own inertia and specific speed, from data sheets or the correlation.
  3. Create three trip scenarios using the pumps' shutdown settings: station 1 alone, the booster alone, both. Add delayed trips for the interlocks with the real delay times.
  4. Report the booster suction and discharge as report points, and plot the profile envelopes over both mains.
  5. For the decoupled option, model the break tank as a tank at its operating level, and size each main's protection with the other station running and tripped.
  6. Check every combination of the final devices against all trips, and the restart order.

7 · Checklist

  • Every station trip modelled alone and together, with the interlocks.
  • Booster suction checked against vapour and NPSH.
  • Bypass and check valves at the booster in the model.
  • Interlock purpose and delay stated, and not relied on for surge protection.
  • Decoupling considered (break tank or reservoir) before protecting an in-line pair as one system.
  • Protection checked against all trips, not only the one it was sized for.
