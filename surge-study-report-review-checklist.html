The Surge Study Report: What to Deliver and What a Reviewer Checks

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 18 min read

A surge study is judged by its report, and reviewers send reports back for the same few reasons: they cannot see the model basis, the profile is missing, results are shown only at the pump, or the protection is described without the settings anyone needs to build it. Here is why the profile matters. The 12 km reference main, with the vessel that protects it on a flat profile (3.5 m³ of gas in a 20 m³ shell), was rerun on an undulating profile with two high points. The pressure history at the pump still looks healthy, never below +4.8 m. Along the line the pressure falls to −5.8 m at the 7.2 km high point, and 8.5 km of main is below the +3.0 m criterion. A report that showed the pump history, or the envelope as head rather than pressure, would have passed it. This article sets out what we put in a surge study, how we present it, and the checklist we use when reviewing someone else's.

1 · What the report has to do

A surge study supports decisions that are expensive to change later: the protection type and size, the pipe classes, the pump and valve specifications, the station layout and the operating rules [1][2]. Its report has three readers, and each needs something different.

  • The reviewer needs to reproduce the reasoning: the model basis, the data and their sources, the assumptions, the scenarios, and evidence that the model was checked.
  • The designers and suppliers need the outputs: device sizes and settings, pressure classes, valve closure times, pump inertia requirements and interlocks.
  • The operator needs the rules: what must never be done, how to restart after a trip, how to maintain the protection.

We write the report so that each reader can find their part without reading the rest, and so that every number in the conclusions can be traced back to a run.

2 · The structure we use

Our surge reports follow the same order on every project. The order matters: the basis and the checks come before any result, so that a reader knows what the results are worth.

5 · Presenting the envelope: pressure along the profile

The envelope is the most important figure in the report and the one most often drawn badly. Three rules:

  • Plot pressure, or plot head together with the pipe profile. A minimum HGL of +20 m tells the reader nothing on a line with a high point at +12 m. A pressure envelope can be read directly against the limits.
  • Show the whole line, with every high point, knee and device chainage marked, and the limits drawn on the same axes.
  • Tabulate the extremes with their chainage, time and governing scenario, so the figure does not have to be read with a ruler.

With the flat-line vessel the head view looks reassuring: the minimum HGL never falls below +4.3 m above datum, everywhere above the pipe on the chart. The pressure view shows what the head view hides: the pressure is −5.2 m at the 2.5 km high point and −5.8 m at 7.2 km. Switch to the design vessel and the lowest pressure is +3.2 m at 2.5 km. The pump reads +12.0 m, nearly four times as much. The maximum is 89.9 m at the pump, well inside the 136 m project limit, because the larger gas volume also softens the upsurge.

6 · Presenting time histories: at the critical points

Time histories show how an event develops: when the minimum arrives, whether it is a single dip or a sustained low, how fast the protection responds. They belong at the points that govern, not only at the pump. We always include the pump or vessel node, every high point that comes within a few metres of the criterion, and the point of the overall minimum and maximum.

A schedule that gives the vessel volume without the gas volume, pressure and connection loss cannot be built to the design; the vessel sizing article and the differential orifice article show how sensitive the result is to each.

8 · Operating rules and interlocks

The protection only works if it is in service and set correctly, so the report ends with rules that go into the operating manual and the control philosophy:

  • Permissives: no pump may start unless the vessel is in service, with level and gas volume within limits.
  • Vessel out of service: the main may not be pumped, or may be pumped only at a reduced flow shown by a run to be safe.
  • Start and stop sequences: pumps start and stop one at a time, at least a stated interval apart, and the line valve closure time is fixed by the run that shows it safe.
  • Restart after a trip: wait until the vessel has refilled and the column has settled; for a line that has drained, follow the filling procedure.
  • Maintenance: check gas volume and connection orifice at stated intervals, and record every trip with a fast pressure logger so the model can be checked against reality.

10 · Why studies are sent back

In our reviews the same problems come up again and again. Most concern presentation and evidence rather than hydraulics:

  • Results only at the pump. The critical point is almost always somewhere else on the line (sections 5 and 6).
  • Head plotted without the profile, so vacuum at a high point is invisible.
  • One scenario. No single pump trip, no start or stop, no protection-out-of-service case.
  • No model checks. No time step, adjustment, halved step or run duration in the report.
  • A peak that rests on cavity collapse accepted against a pressure rating.
  • Assumed pump data presented as fact, with no range tested.
  • A vessel without its gas volume, pressure or connection loss, so it cannot be built as modelled.
  • No operating rules, leaving the protection free to be isolated.
  • Limits without sources: “PN16” with no statement of PFA, PMA or the owner's criteria.

11 · Getting the report out of Bentley HAMMER

Field names differ slightly between versions; these follow the current documentation [4].

  1. Name scenarios and alternatives as they will appear in the report’s scenario matrix, so every figure can carry its scenario name.
  2. Define profiles along the whole main with the ground or pipe level, and plot the maximum and minimum pressure envelopes for each governing scenario.
  3. Set report points at the pump, the vessel and every high point before the run, and turn on Generate Animation Data, so histories exist where they are needed.
  4. Export the extremes with their locations from the transient results tables, and build the extremes table from them rather than reading graphs.
  5. Copy the calculation options into the model basis: time step, adjustment, run duration, friction method and vapour pressure.
  6. Keep the run log with warnings, including any wave-speed or length adjustment warning, and say in the report how each was dealt with.
  7. Archive the model with the report, with a list of scenarios and the run that supports each figure.

12 · Checklist for the author

  • Summary with the governing event, the protection, the margins and the open items.
  • Profile drawing and the weakest component classes.
  • Limits named and referenced, owner's criteria separated from standards.
  • Data and assumptions registers, with every assumption tested or listed as open.
  • Model checks reported.
  • Scenario matrix with the governing run marked for each limit.
  • Pressure envelopes along the whole line and histories at the critical points.
  • Device schedule complete enough to build from.
  • Sensitivity showing that the decision survives the uncertainty.
  • Operating rules and interlocks.
  • Model files archived with the report.
