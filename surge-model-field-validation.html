Validating a Surge Model with a Field Pump-Trip Test: Loggers, Wave Speed and Calibration

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 16 min read

A surge model is a prediction until a real trip has been measured and compared with it. Done properly, the test is short and cheap: two fast pressure loggers, one deliberate pump trip and an afternoon of comparison. Done badly, it proves nothing. A SCADA historian logging once a second can miss 25 m of a 134 m peak, and a model tuned until it matches the wrong trace hides the error it was meant to find. This article works through a pump-trip test on a 4 km main: where to put the transducers, how fast to log, how to read the wave speed straight off the record, and how to calibrate wave speed and pump inertia against the measurement without forcing the fit. The “measured” traces here are synthetic: a model of the same main with a wave speed of 1,000 m/s and an inertia of 50 kg·m², standing in for the real pipe, with logger noise added. That lets every step be checked against a known answer.

1 · Why a test is worth the trouble

Every surge model rests on inputs that are hard to know in advance: the wave speed of a buried pipe with its joints, lining and entrained air, the inertia of a motor and pump that were bought after the study, the real closure of the check valve, and the friction of a pipe in service [1][2]. The sensitivity article shows how far each one moves the answer. A field test replaces the largest of these uncertainties with a measurement. It also tells the operator that the protection works: a vessel whose gas volume has drifted, or an air valve that sticks, shows up in the first recorded trip.

The test main here is the 4 km version of the series' reference line used in Pump data for a pump trip: DN800 ductile iron, 0.70 m³/s, 85.0 m at the pump, a 685 kW set at 1,480 rpm and an ideal check valve. The design model used the catalogue wave speed (1,050 m/s) and the correlation estimate of inertia (44 kg·m²). The “real” pipe has 1,000 m/s and 50 kg·m², a plausible gap on a real project. The design model predicts a lowest head of +1.79 m on the line; the real one reaches +2.65 m, and about +3.0 m at the pump.

2 · Transducers and loggers

  • Where. At the pump discharge downstream of the check valve, at the vessel if there is one, at the high point or knee that governs the minimum, and at one point a known distance along the line, so the wave travel time can be measured. Two well-placed transducers beat six badly placed ones [3].
  • What. Piezoresistive pressure transducers with a range covering both vacuum and the predicted maximum (here −1 to 16 bar), mounted on a short tapping with a full-bore isolating valve, vented of air before the test.
  • How fast. Fast enough to catch the steepest change. A wave front at 1,000 m/s passes a transducer in well under a second, and a cavity collapse in milliseconds. We log at 100 Hz or faster, synchronised between loggers to a common clock [3][4].
  • What else. Pump speed (a tachometer or the drive's speed signal), flow before the trip, reservoir levels and the exact time of the trip.
Why SCADA is not enough A SCADA historian usually stores a value every second or slower, often averaged. Section 3 shows what that does to a peak lasting a fraction of a second. SCADA records are useful to confirm that something happened (see SCADA records in transient analysis); they are not a calibration record.

The minimum, a slower and broader trough on this main, is caught well even at 1 Hz (3.0 to 3.7 m). A cavity collapse would be far sharper than either.

4 · Reading the wave speed off the record

The simplest and most valuable number a test gives is the wave speed, and it needs no model at all. A pump trip sends a downsurge from the pump at the instant of the trip; it reaches a transducer at distance \(x\) after \(x/a\) seconds [1][5]:

\[ a \;=\; \frac{x}{t_{arrival} - t_{trip}}, \qquad \frac{\delta a}{a} \;\approx\; \frac{\delta t}{t_{arrival} - t_{trip}} \]

With a transducer 2 km out and a logger at 100 Hz, the timing uncertainty of one sample (0.01 s) on a travel time of 2.0 s is 0.5 %. At 1 Hz it is 50 %: the measurement is useless. The trip time itself must be known to the same accuracy, which is why the pump-end transducer or the motor current is logged on the same clock.

At 100 Hz with a 1 m threshold the arrival is read at about 2.0 s and the wave speed comes out within 1 % of the true 1,000 m/s: 5 % below the design value. A threshold below the noise triggers on noise; a large threshold waits for the front to deepen and reads it late, biasing the wave speed low.

5 · Calibrating the model without forcing it

With the wave speed measured, the remaining unknowns are adjusted one at a time against the whole trace, not against one peak [2][4][6]. We use the root-mean-square difference over the first 30 s at both transducers as the measure of fit:

\[ \text{RMS} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\big(H_{model}(t_k) - H_{measured}(t_k)\big)^2} \]

The order matters. Fix what the test measures directly (wave speed, steady flow and heads, trip time) first. Then adjust the inputs that are genuinely uncertain and physically bounded (inertia within the range in the data sheets, the pump characteristic between neighbouring curves, the gas volume of a vessel within its level tolerance). Leave alone the things a test cannot identify, such as friction during the transient, unless the steady state is wrong.

The design model (1,050 m/s, 44 kg·m²) is 12.9 m RMS from the record: its waves arrive early and its reflections are out of step. Setting the measured 1,000 m/s drops that to 2.6 m with no other change. Inertia then refines it: 55 kg·m² gives 1.96 m and 35 gives 6.7 m, so the true value (50) is bracketed between the two better runs. No combination at 950 or 1,100 m/s comes within 11 m, however the inertia is set: the wave speed cannot be traded against inertia, which is why it is fixed first.

6 · How calibration goes wrong

  • Tuning to the peak. Matching one maximum by adjusting friction or wave speed can make a wrong model look right. Fit the shape and timing of the whole record.
  • Adjusting what the test cannot see. A trip that never separates the column says nothing about cavity models; a test without a vessel says nothing about its gas law.
  • Hidden air. Free air lowers the wave speed and damps the oscillation. If the measured wave speed is far below the calculated one, look for air before changing the pipe properties (see wave speed).
  • Different initial conditions. The test and the model must start from the same flow, heads and number of pumps running; record them.
  • Testing into danger. The test trip must be one the model already shows to be safe, with protection in service. A test of the unprotected case is never justified.

7 · Test procedure

  1. Agree the test with the operator: the scenario, the pass and abort criteria, who trips the pump and how, and a model run of that exact scenario showing it is safe.
  2. Install and check transducers: range, zero at atmospheric, vented tappings, synchronised clocks, 100 Hz or faster.
  3. Record the steady state for several minutes: flow, heads, speed, levels, vessel level and pressure.
  4. Trip the pump by opening its breaker, as a real power failure would, and keep logging until the line has settled (several minutes on a main with a vessel).
  5. Read the wave speed from the arrival time (section 4) and the steady state from the pre-trip record.
  6. Rerun the model with the measured steady state, then adjust the uncertain inputs within their bounds, judging each step by the RMS over the whole record.
  7. Report the calibrated values and their effect on the design envelope, and keep the record for comparison after any change to the system.

8 · Doing it in Bentley HAMMER

Field names differ slightly between versions [7].

  1. Build a test scenario that matches the test exactly: the pumps running, the initial flow and levels from the pre-trip record.
  2. Place report points at the transducer locations, and set the report period to every time step so the model trace has the same resolution as the logger.
  3. Import or overlay the measured trace on the time-history graph of each report point (export both to a spreadsheet if your version cannot overlay).
  4. Set wave speeds from the measured value, pipe by pipe or as a global factor, and check the steady state still matches.
  5. Create alternatives for the uncertain inputs (inertia, specific-speed curve, vessel gas volume) and a scenario for each, and compare the RMS.
  6. Carry the calibrated values back into the design scenarios and rerun the envelope.

9 · Checklist

  • Test scenario modelled and shown safe before it is run.
  • Transducers at the pump, the critical point and a known distance, logging at 100 Hz or faster on a common clock.
  • Steady state recorded before the trip and used in the model.
  • Wave speed read from the arrival time, with its timing uncertainty.
  • Calibration on the whole record (RMS), one physically bounded input at a time.
  • Calibrated values reported, with their effect on the design envelope.
