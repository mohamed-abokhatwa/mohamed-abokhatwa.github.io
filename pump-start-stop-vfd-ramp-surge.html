Pump Starts, Controlled Stops and Drive Ramps: Sizing Normal Operations from the Surge Model

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 14 min read

A power failure stops a pump in a second or two; a variable-speed drive can take as long as the designer asks. On the unprotected 12 km reference main, ramping the pump to zero speed in 10 s still takes the line to vapour; 20 s leaves it at −5.0 m; 31.2 s, about 1.4 times 2L/a, is the shortest ramp that keeps every point at +3.0 m or above. Starting the same pump is almost harmless here, whatever the ramp: the head never exceeds about 102.5 m, because a pump cannot deliver more than its shut-off head. This article shows how to size stop and start ramps and pump control valve times from the model, why the flow stops long before the speed reaches zero, and which interlocks to write, while remembering that none of it helps when the power fails.

1 · Normal operations are surge events too

A pumped main sees a few power failures a year and thousands of normal starts and stops. Each normal event is a transient, and three things make them matter [1][2]:

  • They are recurring. On plastic pipes their pressure swings set the fatigue life (see the pipe's real limits).
  • They are controllable. Their rate is a setting on a drive or a valve, so the surge study can fix it.
  • They can be worse than expected. A controlled stop that is too quick is a pump trip with extra steps.

The examples use the series' reference main without protection: 12 km DN800 ductile iron, 2L/a = 22.9 s, a pump with a 100 m shut-off rise above a 5 m suction, delivering 0.70 m³/s against a 44.8 m reservoir [3]. The network solver prescribes the pump speed (a drive ramp) or the opening of a pump control valve; it uses a vapour cavity model and has been verified against the series solver to 0.01 m.

The shortest ramp is found between the computed points by interpolation; at +3.0 m a bisection on the model itself gives 31.2 s. Ramps of 0 and 10 s take the line to vapour, 20 s to −5.0 m, 40 s to +4.4 m. Beyond about two minutes each extra minute buys only a few metres. The maximum never exceeds the steady 85 m: a controlled stop of a pump with a check valve has no upsurge of its own.

3 · The flow stops long before the speed does

A centrifugal pump cannot deliver against a head higher than its shut-off head at that speed, which falls with the square of the speed. The pump stops lifting water into the line when [4][5]

\[ H_{suc} + H_0\,w^2 = H_{res} \quad\Rightarrow\quad w_0 = \sqrt{\frac{H_{res} - H_{suc}}{H_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{44.8 - 5.0}{100}} = 0.631 \]

On a linear ramp to zero, the pump has done its useful work after the first 37 % of the ramp: a 60 s ramp is in effect a 22 s deceleration of the column. Drives that ramp to a minimum speed rather than to zero, and pumps with a high static lift, make this worse. When specifying a stop ramp, think of the time from full speed to \(w_0\), not to zero; some drives allow a two-stage ramp (fast to just above \(w_0\), slow through it) for exactly this reason.

4 · Starting: drive ramp or pump control valve

A start accelerates the column from rest. A drive ramps the speed up; a fixed-speed pump starts direct on line against a pump control valve that opens over a set time. In both cases the pump's head cannot exceed its shut-off head, 105 m at the pump here, so the start cannot produce a maximum above that unless air or vapour is in the line. On this main both kinds of start, at every ramp or opening time from 1 to 90 s, give a maximum of 99–102.5 m and no fall below the static 44.8 m [2][6].

The start's maximum is set by the shut-off head, not by the ramp: a slower ramp only delays the column's acceleration. The real risk at a start is elsewhere: air at high points after a drain-down (see filling and restart), a check valve that opens late and lets the column slam it open, or starting into a column still oscillating after the previous stop.

5 · Settings and interlocks to write from the model

  • Stop ramp: at least the shortest ramp that passes, with margin (31.2 s here; we would set 45 s), applied to every normal stop, including stops by level control.
  • Emergency stop: a coast stop is a trip; the protection must cover it, not the ramp.
  • Start ramp or valve opening: long enough to keep starting current and air release under control; the surge seldom sets it.
  • Minimum time between a stop and the next start, long enough for the column to settle.
  • Pumps in parallel: stop and start them one at a time, with the stop ramp applying to each.
  • A power failure is not covered by any of these. The trip remains the case that sizes the protection (see choosing surge protection).

6 · Setting it up in Bentley HAMMER

Field names differ slightly between versions [7].

  1. Model the controlled stop as a variable-speed pump with a speed pattern ramping from 1 to 0 over the ramp time (or the pump's own speed-controlled transient setting in your version), not as a pump shutdown, which HAMMER treats as a trip.
  2. Model the start as a pump start with a speed ramp, or as a pump with a downstream control valve whose opening follows the specified time.
  3. Create one scenario per ramp and use batch run to bracket the shortest ramp that passes.
  4. Start from the right initial state: a full line at rest for a start, the duty point for a stop.
  5. Record the recurring pressure swing at the critical points for the fatigue check of plastic pipes.
  6. Write the settings into the control philosophy with the scenario that justifies each.

7 · Checklist

  • Stop ramp sized from the model, with margin, and judged on the time to the zero-flow speed.
  • Start checked against the shut-off head and for air and late check valve opening.
  • Pump control valve times set from the model where valves are used.
  • Emergency stop and power failure covered by the protection, not by settings.
  • Recurring swings passed to the fatigue check.
  • Settings and interlocks written into the control philosophy.
