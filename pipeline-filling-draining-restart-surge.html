Filling, Draining and Restarting a Transmission Main: Air Valve Slam, Trapped Air and Safe Rates

Mohamed Abokhatwa | Mechanical Manager · MSc, PMP, PMI-RMP, Envision SP | November 2026 | 18 min read

More transmission mains are damaged while they are being filled or restarted than during the pump trip their surge study was written for. On the 12 km reference main, filling at 0.3 m/s means a closing air valve stops the column with a Joukowsky rise of 32 m; at 2 m/s the same closure gives 214 m, above the line's 136 m limit. Opening a filling valve fully from a 40 m head into a 1 km leg that holds 50 m of trapped air drives that pocket to 279 m, seven times the driving head. Throttle the same fill to 0.3 m/s and the pocket peaks at 40.5 m. This article sets out the physics, the rates and the air valve checks that keep filling, draining and restarting inside the limits, and what a full-pipe surge program such as HAMMER can and cannot show.

1 · Why filling is a surge event

A pipe being filled is not full, and a pipe being drained is not empty. Both contain moving water and moving air, and air behaves very differently from water: it is 800 times lighter, a thousand times more compressible, and it escapes through an orifice far faster than water can follow it. Three mechanisms cause most of the damage [1][2][3]:

  • Air valve slam. While air is leaving through a large orifice, the water behind it can reach high velocity. When the water arrives at the valve, the float shuts and the column stops abruptly.
  • Trapped air compressed by an accelerating column. A pocket that cannot escape acts as a spring. A column started suddenly from a high head overshoots and squeezes the pocket to several times the driving head.
  • Restarting into a partly drained line. After a trip or a drain-down, voids at high points refill at the full velocity of a running pump.

The reference main of the surge series is a 12 km DN800 ductile iron line with a wave speed of 1,050 m/s, a steady HGL of 85.0 m at the pump and a 136 m project limit [4]. It holds 6,032 m³ of water.

2 · Filling velocity and air valve slam

When the water reaches an air valve, the float closes and the column that was moving at velocity \(v\) stops in a time much shorter than the wave travel time. The head rise is the Joukowsky value [5]:

\[ \Delta H \;=\; \frac{a\,v}{g}, \qquad 0.3\ \text{m/s:}\ \frac{1050 \times 0.3}{9.81} = 32.1\ \text{m}, \qquad 2.0\ \text{m/s:}\ 214.1\ \text{m} \]

The velocity that matters is the velocity of the water arriving at the valve, and while air is leaving freely that is close to the filling velocity. In a plastic pipe with a wave speed near 300 m/s, the same velocities give 9.2 and 61.2 m. The commonly quoted filling limit of about 0.3 m/s (1 ft/s), found in AWWA guidance on air valves, keeps the slam of a ductile iron main near 30 m [1].

The other half of the check is the air valve itself. During filling, the free-air flow out of the valve equals the water flow in. Through an orifice the air needs a pressure difference to leave, and isentropic orifice flow gives [6]

\[ \dot m \;=\; C_d A\,p_0\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{(\gamma-1)RT_0}\left[\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)^{2/\gamma}-\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/\gamma}\right]} \]

with \(C_d\) = 0.6, \(\gamma\) = 1.4 and the pressure ratio no lower than the critical 0.528, beyond which the flow is choked. Filling the reference main at 0.3 m/s pushes 0.151 m³/s of air out. A DN100 large orifice passes that at 0.006 bar; a DN50 orifice needs 0.10 bar with the air leaving at 77 m/s. Too small an orifice slows the fill; too large an orifice on a fast fill lets the column accelerate into the valve. That is why anti-slam and two-stage air valves throttle the last of the air, and why the filling rate, not the valve size alone, sets the slam [1][7].

The verdict compares the slam head, added to the static head of a filled line at rest (44.8 m here), with the 136 m project limit, and flags an air exhaust pressure above 0.1 bar. Filling the reference main at 0.3 m/s takes 11.1 hours; at 1 m/s it takes 3.3 hours, but the slam is 107 m. Shorter sections between valves let each fill faster at the same velocity, because the time is set by the volume, not by the rate limit.

3 · Trapped air: the pocket that becomes a spring

Where air cannot escape, for example at a high point with no air valve or a closed valve at the end of a section, the arriving column compresses it. Treating the water as a rigid column, which is valid while the pocket is large compared with the elastic storage of the pipe, gives a pair of equations that every analyst should be able to solve by hand [2][8][9]:

\[ \frac{dv}{dt} \;=\; \frac{g}{L_c}\left(H_R - H_p - K\frac{v|v|}{2g}\right) - \frac{f\,v|v|}{2D}, \qquad \frac{dV}{dt} = -A\,v, \qquad H_p V^{n} = H_{atm}V_0^{\,n} \]

Heads are absolute: \(H_R\) is the driving head plus the atmosphere (10.33 m), \(H_p\) the pocket pressure, \(L_c\) the column length, \(K\) the loss coefficient of the filling valve or throttle, and \(n\) the gas law exponent. The pocket starts at atmospheric pressure with the column at rest.

Take a 1 km DN800 leg filled from a 40 m head through a valve opened fully, with 50 m of pipe (25 m³) of trapped air at its closed end. The column accelerates to 3.76 m/s, overshoots, and the pocket peaks at 278.5 m gauge 19 s after opening: seven times the driving head and twice the project limit of this series. A throttle sized for a steady filling velocity of 0.3 m/s keeps the column near that velocity, and the pocket rises slowly to 40.5 m, barely above the driving head.

Shrink the pocket and the rigid-column peak keeps rising: 398.5 m for 20 m of air and 521.1 m for 2 m. Those small-pocket numbers are not real. A small pocket stops the column within a few metres, at a low velocity, and the elasticity of the water and the pipe then limits the peak to about the water hammer value at that velocity (97 m for the 2 m pocket). The two curves cross near 30 m of air on this leg, and that is where the worst real peaks occur: the pocket is small enough to stop the column hard and large enough to let it reach speed first. Use the lower curve as a screening value, and an elastic model with a gas pocket (section 6) for design.

4 · Draining: let the air in

Draining reverses the problem. Water leaving through a washout must be replaced by air entering through the air valves at the high points, or the pressure there falls towards vacuum and the column separates. The same orifice equation, with atmospheric air flowing into a pipe below atmospheric pressure, sizes the inflow. Draining the reference main through a washout at 0.10 m³/s needs 0.10 m³/s of free air at the high points: a DN50 orifice admits 0.19 m³/s at 0.2 bar of vacuum, a DN100 admits 0.76. The same inflow capacity protects the line when a pump trip separates the column, which is the case air valves are usually sized for [1][10].

5 · Restarting after a trip or a drain-down

After a power failure the column may have separated at high points, and after a long stop the line may have drained partly through leaks, washouts or air valves. Restarting a pump at full speed into such a line repeats sections 2 and 3 at pump velocity: the column runs into the voids at 1–2 m/s, and the air valves at the high points close on it. Our restart procedure:

  • Wait for the line to settle after a trip: the vessel refilled, cavities collapsed, air valves closed. On the reference main the column swings for several minutes (see run duration).
  • Refill slowly first where the line may have drained: a bypass round the pump, the pump at minimum speed on its drive, or the pump control valve held at a small opening, with the filling velocity kept near 0.3 m/s until the high points have vented.
  • Interlock the restart: no full-speed start until pressure at the high points is positive, and a minimum time after any trip.
  • Check the air valves after every drain-down, since a stuck float turns a vented high point into a trapped pocket.

6 · What HAMMER can and cannot show

HAMMER, like other full-pipe method-of-characteristics programs, assumes every pipe is full of liquid along its length. It cannot simulate an empty pipe filling, a stratified flow running down a slope, or air travelling along the pipe [11]. It can model gas at nodes, and that covers most of what a designer needs:

  1. Air valves at high points, with their inflow and outflow orifice sizes and, for triple-acting or anti-slam valves, the switching settings. Use them to check slam on restart and the vacuum during a trip.
  2. A trapped pocket at a node, represented by a small gas volume (for example a vessel element at the high point with no connection loss) at its expected size and pressure. Run the restart with that pocket in place to get an elastic answer for section 3.
  3. The restart itself: a pump start with the real ramp or valve opening (see normal operations) into a line whose initial condition has the pockets in place.
  4. The filling procedure is then checked by hand with the rigid-column model of section 3 and the slam limit of section 2, and written into the operating manual with rates, valve sequences and hold points.

7 · Checklist

  • Filling velocity set (about 0.3 m/s unless a calculation supports more), and the slam head checked against the pipe's limits.
  • Air valves sized for exhaust during filling and inflow during draining, with the manufacturer's tested curves.
  • Anti-slam or two-stage valves where filling or restart velocities cannot be kept low.
  • Every high point vented, or the trapped pocket checked with the rigid-column model and an elastic model.
  • Filling valve or bypass sized as a throttle, not opened fully against a closed or air-filled section.
  • Restart procedure with a settling time, a slow refill and an interlock on high-point pressure.
  • Procedure in the operating manual, with rates, sequences and hold points.
